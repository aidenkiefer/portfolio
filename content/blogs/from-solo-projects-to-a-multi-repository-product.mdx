On a solo project, I have a very convenient source of architectural context: the person who made all the decisions is usually me.

Sometimes that person left helpful notes. Sometimes he named something `final` and moved on with his life. Either way, I can usually reconstruct what happened.

I joined OPENLANE’s LaneLink team in late June, and working full-time on an existing product has started to stretch that habit. I’m learning to understand a change as part of a larger workflow, even when the relevant behavior lives in several places.

The following examples are simplified and fictional. The lessons are personal; the systems described here are illustrative.

## The repository is one piece of the map

In my [earlier post about unfamiliar codebases](/blog/what-i-look-for-first-in-an-unfamiliar-codebase), I focused on getting oriented: finding entry points, following data, and understanding what persists. Those questions still help. The next challenge is following the thread when it leaves the codebase I started in.

For a generic web product, a user action might involve an interface, a service, stored information, and a later background update. Each part can look reasonable on its own while the complete experience is confusing.

That has changed where I start. Before looking for a file to edit, I try to describe what the person using the software is trying to accomplish. Then I follow that action far enough to understand which boundaries matter.

My mental map now includes the spaces between the boxes.

## A small button can have a long story

Auction transfer work has been a useful example of this kind of reasoning. The general idea sounds straightforward: move something from one auction to another.

To keep the example separate from work, imagine a small community auction app. A donated bicycle was assigned to Saturday’s sale, but a volunteer needs to move it to the following week.

The visible action might be a single button. The questions behind it are more interesting. What should the volunteer see afterward? What should remain visible about the earlier assignment? Could another part of the application still be showing the previous information?

I can’t answer those questions by looking at the button alone. I need to understand the meaning of the action across the whole experience.

The button has excellent branding. The complexity is doing its best to remain anonymous.

## Understanding where it runs matters too

I’m also paying more attention to the difference between code being correct in isolation and a change working in its intended environment.

In a hypothetical application, an interactive feature and a background task may run under different conditions. A local walkthrough can tell me something useful about the interface without answering whether a later action will happen as expected.

That doesn’t mean every task needs an infrastructure expedition. It means my confidence should match what I actually checked. If I only exercised one part of a workflow, I should be able to say which part.

“I saw the screen update” is a useful observation. It leaves some questions open.

## Asking a smaller, better question

With personal projects, I can disappear into research until the whole design feels comfortable. On an existing product, trying to understand everything before contributing would be a very effective way to contribute nothing.

I’m learning to work with a bounded slice: enough context to explain the behavior I’m changing, the assumptions it depends on, and the parts I still need help understanding.

That also makes my questions more useful. “Can you explain the entire application?” asks someone else to organize my uncertainty. “I traced the action this far; am I misunderstanding what happens next?” gives us something concrete to discuss.

There is still plenty I don’t know. Having a more precise description of it feels like progress.

## A different kind of ownership

I still like the freedom of building an application from scratch. Working in an existing product is developing a different skill: making a focused change while understanding enough of the surrounding system to respect it.

A month in, I’m spending more effort connecting the user’s action to the behavior around it. I’m also getting more comfortable explaining the limits of what I understand.

My solo projects taught me to build the pieces. This experience is teaching me to notice the joins.
